Acceptable use

riskscope is a defensive tool. It exists to help people find and fix security problems on systems they are responsible for.

Only scan what you're allowed to

Run riskscope against a website, IP address or codebase only if one of these is true:

  • you own it, or your organisation does; or
  • you have explicit, written permission from the owner to test it (for example a signed penetration-test scope); or
  • you are a national CERT or equivalent body acting within your legal mandate.

Scanning systems you don't control, without the owner's authorisation, is illegal in most countries — including under Tunisia's Law No. 2004-5 on cyber security and the Penal Code's computer-crime provisions — even when you intend to help. Good intentions are not consent: the owner, not the scanner, decides.

What riskscope will and won't do

  • Website checks that behave like an ordinary visitor or search engine (headers, certificates, public DNS, well-known file paths) run on any public site.
  • Anything more intrusive — port scans, and scanning more than a single small block at a time — is deliberately limited, and the more active checks require you to prove you own the domain first. These limits are intentional and are not a bug to work around.
  • riskscope is not built for mass-scanning address space you don't own, and we won't add features whose purpose is to do that.

If you want to protect others

The lawful way to help organisations you don't control is coordinated disclosure: use already-published internet-scan data (such as Shodan, Censys or Rapid7 Open Data) to identify exposure, then contact the owner or their national CERT — without sending scanning traffic yourself. In Tunisia that is ANSI / tunCERT.

Abuse

Scans are rate-limited per visitor. Operators of a riskscope instance may block or throttle any source that appears to be scanning third parties without authorisation.
